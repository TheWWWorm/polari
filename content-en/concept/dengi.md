Polari's money is minted in gold, silver, and copper, with fixed ratios between denominations. All calculations ultimately come down to the **agatka**, a small silver coin: a gloria equals eight agatkas, an elena equals eight glorias, and a hilt equals four elenas. The coins' names refer to the Empire's symbols and its holy [[pramateri|Foremothers]].

## Coins
From the highest denomination to the lowest:
- **Hilt** — a gold coin, or a “gold piece.” It bears an image of the grip of the [[vechnyj-efes|Eternal Hilt]], which gives it its name. Its sheen is “yellow with a glint of gold,” so thieves call hilts “yellowboys.” A hilt = 4 elenas = 32 glorias = 256 agatkas: a pilgrim could feed himself for two months on one. Large sums, wages, and the prices of Objects are reckoned in hilts. Harmon, for example, hoped to sell the [[svetlaya-sfera|Radiant Sphere]] for at least thirty thousand hilts, while a kair's pay was five gold pieces a month.
- **Elena** — a large silver coin bearing a lantern, an attribute of Foremother [[pramater-elena|Elena]], hence its nickname “lantern.” An elena = 8 glorias = 64 agatkas: admission to the flower festival in the capital cost “an elena—sixty-four agatkas.”
- **Gloria** — a silver coin bearing the profile of Saint [[pramater-gloriya|Gloria]]; the Foremother's “magnificent big eyes” earned it the nickname “little owl.” A gloria = 8 agatkas.
- **Agatka** — a small silver coin and the basic unit of everyday transactions. Its obverse shows a young woman, [[pramater-agata|Agatha]] the Radiant; its reverse bears a goose feather, her symbol, hence the nickname “feather.” Amounts are also reckoned in “half an agatka.”
- **Poltinka** (poltina) — half an agatka: in [[konfidentka|The Confidante]], Josh produces “half an agatka” and calls it a “poltinka.”
- **Little star** — a copper coin, or a “copper.” Little stars are given as change, handed to beggars, thrown to street performers, and used in card games played “for small stakes”; a mug of beer may cost one and a half little stars. The expression “not a little star” means “not a penny.” The books do not say how many little stars make an agatka.

## How Money Is Counted
- **In eights.** Among the silver coins, each step is eightfold: 8 agatkas make a gloria, and 8 glorias make an elena. Gold alone breaks the sequence: a hilt equals four elenas. In a discussion of the world's “even numbers,” “eight agatkas in a gloria” appears alongside two sexes, two continents, and four cardinal directions.
- **Sums are stated in coins of different denominations**, from large to small: “an elena and two glorias,” or “three elenas, one gloria, and four agatkas.” Harmon calculated the cost of his skyship down to the last gloria: four hundred and twenty-three hilts, two elenas, and five glorias.
- **Stakes descend through the denominations.** Of a game in which the stakes kept shrinking, it is said that they first played for an elena, then for a gloria, then for agatkas, and now for poltinkas.
- **Examples confirming the rates in the books.** Twenty-four agatkas are “three full-weight silver glorias.” “Sixteen agatkas are two glorias,” a schoolgirl answers during a fractions lesson in [[fantazii|Fantasies]]. Paying with a hilt, Alanis points out that a hilt is worth two hundred and fifty-six agatkas.

## Changing and Exchanging Money
Coins of different metals are exchanged at fixed rates. A whole hilt that has not been changed into smaller coins is conspicuous: someone paying with gold in a poor place gives himself away, since somebody will have to change the hilt, which means showing it.

In poor regions, such as the remote parts of the [[yuzhnyj-put|Southern Way]], money scarcely circulated. Peasants bartered goods for goods, copper little stars made up the difference “in an unequal exchange,” and silver agatkas, glorias, and especially elenas seemed exotic. For the old soldier [[doksett|Doxett]], a couple of silver agatkas were “almost a fortune.”

## Coin Quality
- **Juliana's elenas.** Platinum was added to the silver of elenas minted under [[yuliana-velikaya|Juliana the Great]], so the old coins hardly tarnish. People say of an old woman trying to look young, “Here comes Juliana's elena!”
- **Counterfeiters.** They are executed by having molten lead poured into their mouths. Among the suspicious items found during raids in the capital in the spring of 1775 was a pound of poor-quality counterfeit money.

## Prices
Several prices from the text provide a basis for comparison.
- **Small purchases.** Harmon bought a small mirror for eight agatkas. A cup of coffee cost two agatkas—“more expensive than wine.” A bowl of goulash at a station tavern cost [[dzhoakin|Joaquin]] half an agatka, which was considered expensive. Fortune-tellers at the festival charged half an agatka for a prediction. A bucket of herring bought wholesale in the North costs five and a half agatkas.
- **Goods.** A crafty trader offered a mirror “straight from Faunterra” for an elena, or one “almost the same” for three glorias. An impoverished baron boasted that a Hollywell war stallion had cost him fifty hilts. Tuition at a cheap boarding school costs fifty hilts; at a “tolerable” one, two to three hundred.
- **Living expenses.** Twenty hilts per man meant “a couple of years of comfortable living” to the soldiers. In the spring of 1775, when the capital was being searched for the Puppeteer, an informer received a gloria for reporting travellers with suspicious baggage, or two glorias for reporting an armed group.
- **Land and property.** By Harmon's estimate, a village costs between five hundred and fifteen hundred hilts, a two-masted schooner a couple of thousand, and a good, solid castle about ten thousand. A third-class train ticket cost two or three elenas, while a ticket in the very highest class from Labelin to the capital cost nine hilts. Harmon spent four hundred and twenty-three hilts, two elenas, and five glorias on his skyship—more than he had saved in his entire life before selling the Sphere.
- **Sacred relics and weapons.** A single [[svyashchennye-predmety|Sacred Object]] cost tens of thousands of hilts: according to Shayland, Duke Labelin bought an Object for thirty-six thousand gold pieces. A double Sphere was valued at no less than three thousand. A “cypress”-class spark “eye” cost two hundred hilts.
- **The state.** Maintaining a third of Origin's army cost 150 thousand hilts a year. Building the railways planned by [[adrian|Adrian]] required “more than a million hilts” over and above existing expenditure. To fund it, the Crown introduced a universal tax of one-tenth of income (see [[reformy-adriana|Adrian's Reforms]]). By Minerva's estimate, extending the “wave” between all the major cities would cost hundreds of thousands of hilts.

## Bills of Exchange and Banks
Large sums are transported as **bills of exchange**—written debt instruments that banks accept for payment. Money is deposited in a bank in one city and collected in another against a bill made out to a named recipient. The bank charges one coin out of every hundred for the service: one percent. In 1775, Erwin compensated a plundered Shavan village with a bill that “will be accepted for payment at any bank in the central lands,” while the captain of the foraging company issued bills for requisitioned food. The Counts of [[dom-shejland|Shayland]] are former bankers, and [[kharmon|Harmon]] kept his money in their bank.

Early in 1775, Empress [[minerva|Minerva]] received the bankers Mr Konto of First Credit, Mr Ferguson, and Ms Day. Together they offered the Crown two hundred thousand hilts at twenty-five percent annual interest—“a hundred thousand from each bank”—and the same amount again a month later. Minerva did not know whether an “annual quarter” was a good deal, but the idea of paper money captivated her. Ferguson and Day had invested large sums in Adrian's reforms on three occasions: its money had laid “more than a hundred miles of rails.” Ferguson dreamed of a single network of banks connected by the “wave,” allowing money to be sent from one end of the continent to the other in a day.

## Banknotes
Konto and Day showed Minerva **banknotes**—bearer instruments printed in coloured inks, with fixed denominations of one hundred hilts each. A note could be exchanged for silver or gold at any branch of the bank, or used to make a payment. According to Konto, the invention had existed for about five years, since printing technology had made such complex printing possible. Ferguson admitted the catch: townspeople distrusted paper, and ordinary folk would accept only silver.

### The Spring Fair of 1775
At a reception for wine-makers, Minerva played the part of a flighty young woman and announced that she wanted a spring fair on Palace Island. All trading there would use banknotes, each bearing her name. A paper gloria would be sold for seven silver agatkas instead of eight, with the Crown covering the remaining eighth. Likewise, a paper elena would cost seven glorias in silver rather than eight. These paired prices also confirm that an elena contains eight glorias. The bankers tried to object that they could not print so many notes in three weeks.

After the reception, the Empress took Konto into her carriage, and he worked out her plan. The Crown would charge merchants for the right to trade on the island—an agatka from each gloria of revenue—and thus lose nothing on selling the notes cheaply. About half a million hilts in paper would enter circulation; Konto calculated that some two hundred thousand would stay in circulation, making the Empire richer by that amount. Minerva also asked Konto for a secret bank account known only to the two of them. Konto realised that she was creating “an alternative treasury, unaccountable to Robert Origin.”

### Aftermath
The venture proved profitable. Soon afterwards, Minerva told her lady-in-waiting that the paper-money idea “had worked unexpectedly well” and instructed Konto's bank to pay the lady-in-waiting five thousand hilts. She credited her assistants, Finance Minister [[albert-vial|Albert Vial]] and Tax Minister Frank Morlin-May, with introducing banknotes. Walking through the capital's market with Rebecca, she confirmed that paper money “remained in circulation”; the Finance Minister later issued further batches. Erwin's imaginary companion, the [[alter|altessa]] Anxiety, listed paper money among Minerva's chief achievements during her first three months on the throne.

## Taxes
Traditionally, only the Crownlands pay a regular tax to the Dynasty; the Great Houses fulfil their vassal obligations through military service. Taxes are customarily paid by the Day of Plenty. In 1775, the former minister of taxes and duties, [[drejfus-born|Dreyfus Born]], confessed to enormous embezzlement. The new minister, Morlin-May, announced a reform: every farm, workshop, and guild in the Crownlands would be assessed a fixed annual tax, and a bank branch would open in every town to receive the payments. Minerva explained to Treasurer [[robert-oridzhin|Robert Origin]] that the purpose was to eliminate tax collectors, “a source of greed, cruelty, and theft.” The banks of Ferguson, Day, and Konto would transfer the treasury's due share every month. Minerva later included “an honest tax and paper money” among her achievements.

## The Author's Articles
In 2018, Roman Surzhikov wrote articles for Polaripedia—the series' Fandom wiki—about four coins: the [agatka](https://polari.fandom.com/ru/wiki/%D0%90%D0%B3%D0%B0%D1%82%D0%B0_%28%D0%BC%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B0%29), [gloria](https://polari.fandom.com/ru/wiki/%D0%93%D0%BB%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%B8%D1%8F_%28%D0%BC%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B0%29), [little star](https://polari.fandom.com/ru/wiki/%D0%97%D0%B2%D1%91%D0%B7%D0%B4%D0%BE%D1%87%D0%BA%D0%B0_%28%D0%BC%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B0%29), and [hilt](https://polari.fandom.com/ru/wiki/%D0%AD%D1%84%D0%B5%D1%81_%28%D0%BC%D0%BE%D0%BD%D0%B5%D1%82%D0%B0%29). They add the following details to the novels.

- **Shapes and mottoes.** Coins can be distinguished by touch: a little star is round, an agatka is hexagonal with a milled edge, and glorias and elenas are octagonal. The agatka bears the inscription “St. Forem. Agatha the Radiant” and the motto “Not the deed, but the decision”—an imprecise shortening of Agatha's words, “Courage lies not in the deed, but in the decision.” The reverse of a gloria shows a shield tied with a kerchief and the motto “By sharing, you multiply,” paraphrasing Gloria the Protectress's words about goodness shared with one's neighbour. The hilt shows the upper part of the blade and the grip of the [[vechnyj-efes|Eternal Hilt]]; on the reverse are the Dynasty's feather and sword, with the inscription “For ever and ever.” A little star has no motto: it bears an eight-pointed star and the sacred spiral.
- **Nicknames.** The gloria acquired the nickname “little owl” because of a minting defect under Tellurian: the Foremother's eyes came out too large, as though she were frightened. In criminal slang, the agatka is a “little feather,” after the image on its reverse. The books use the corresponding phrase: “not a feather in one's pocket.”
- **Small change.** A little star is 1/16 of an agatka. Its larger counterparts are also minted in copper: the quarter, or quarta, worth 4 little stars (a quarter of an agatka), and the poltinka, worth 8 little stars. They bear the numerals 4 and 8 in place of the star. The quarta also appears in the books: in [[konfidentka|The Confidante]], Josh has a poltinka, while Marek has “a quarta.” The books do not, however, call the poltinka copper: Mira, who carried no copper coins, kept “poltinkas, agatkas, and glorias” in her purse.
- **The agatka with a hole.** Early agatkas of Miriamite mintage had a hole so they could be worn around the neck. The Brilliant Dynasty abandoned it because the hole disfigured the Foremother's face.
- **What it can buy.** In the North, an agatka buys a night's lodging at an inn with breakfast, a filling meal for two, or a bottle of decent orj. Hence the saying “There's only an agatka's worth of work here!” for an easy, quick task. In [[shimmeri|Shimmery]], it buys half an hour in a boat or iced tea with sweets; in [[faunterra|Faunterra]], it will scarcely pay for a cab from the outskirts to the centre.
- **The threshold of comfort.** Almost everywhere except Faunterra, a family with several children can live comfortably for a day on a gloria and still put aside an agatka or two. According to the author, an income of one gloria a day marks the threshold of the middle class “as we understand it.” A hilt is a successful craftsman's monthly income, and a person carrying a whole hilt is no longer poor.
- **The history of the hilt.** Six hundred years ago, coins were minted by the Great Houses: agatkas by [[dom-oridzhin|Origin]], glorias by the Marquesses of Greysend, elenas by the Counts of [[blekmor|Blackmore]], and gold by the Dukes of [[dom-lajthart|Lightheart]] in the [[serdtse-sveta|Heart of Light]]. The twelve-sided gold coin, bearing the duke's portrait and a cup beneath a rainbow, was called a “cup.” At the end of the XII century, Olgard the Founder standardised the monetary system and established a mint in Faunterra. The Houses retained their minting rights, subject to Crown standards. Today, hilts are also minted in [[shimmeri|Shimmery]] and [[darkvoter|Darkwater]]: instead of the feather and sword, their reverses show a lion on a rock and a snake winding around a tree branch.
- **A discrepancy with the books.** The article says the hilt has neither nicknames nor sayings associated with it, since ordinary people never handle gold. In the novels, however, thieves call hilts “yellowboys.”

## In Fantasies
[[fantazii|Fantasies]], stories about the characters' possible futures in 1776–1777, provides the following information about money.
- Under the tax reform originally conceived by Adrian and supported by Minerva, every working resident of Polaris pays between sixteen and thirty-two percent of income to the treasury, depending on occupation. Many believed Minerva had devised the idea of paying taxes through banks, although her people insisted that it was Adrian's idea.
- According to High Mother Alicia, Minerva's reform freed people from the collectors' arbitrary abuses, but the tax rate remained unchanged; taxes had already reached “alarming heights” under Tellurian. [[adrian|Adrian]], needing money for the election and receiving no loans from Duke Labelin, commissioned a printing house to produce paper money. Alicia warned of inflation: once there were too many banknotes, “they will turn from gold into copper,” prices would rise, and the new emperor would have to raise taxes. The Chapter's financiers calculated that, with military and court expenditure at their existing levels, no ruler would be able to cut taxes. Erwin swore by Agatha not to raise them and decided to ask Minerva what “inflation” meant.
- Banknotes were in use: [[moris-labelin|Maurice Labelin]] thrust a bundle worth “about five hundred hilts” at Professor [[nikolas-olli|Ollie]] and promised ten thousand for his device.
- Minerva received a loan from Mother Cordelia, which part of the Chapter called a bribe.
- Duke Farway-Almera paid retired soldiers a pension of four glorias a month. The father of [[salli|Sally the schoolgirl]] drank away half of it, and Sally managed the entire household on the remaining sixteen agatkas, at half an agatka a day.
- Prices: in Nen-Clair, a potion to end infatuation costs a hilt; in Laem, it was sold for fifty. Maintaining the Maidens' Palace in Laem cost [[demetra-nellis-lajon|Demetra]] seventy hilts a month. The lawman Mac—Macfrid Crow, evidently [[mark-voron|Mark]]—tried to persuade the executioner Walter Jane, who had come to Finborough to execute a dog, that the only shame lay in how easily the money could be earned: “a whole hilt for a minute's work.”

## Trivia
- In early editions of [[strela-moneta-iskra|Arrow, Coin, Spark]], admission to the flower festival cost “two elenas—sixty-four agatkas,” leading readers to infer an exchange rate of 32 agatkas to the elena. In the 2026 edition, the phrase was corrected to “an elena—sixty-four agatkas,” consistent with the paper elena sold for seven glorias in [[kukla-na-trone|A Doll on the Throne]].
- In Fantasies, Minerva calculated the cost of the Empire's services in agatkas: one agatka a week from every adult Polarian would be enough to meet all the state's needs.
- Even a loan of a million hilts would not have made it possible to build the dam beyond the River: this was how Erwin realised that the explorada of 1774 had been pointless.
- Robert Origin served as Treasurer of First Winter for six years. The treasury grew poorer under him, but, as Erwin assured Minerva, “not a single agatka was stolen.”
