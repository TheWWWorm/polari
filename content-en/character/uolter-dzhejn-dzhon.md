## Who He Is

Walter Jane John is an executioner, the "master of the executable guild." He is six feet tall and so wide in his shoulders that a barrel enters the door of the dungeon; the face is framed by a billowing beard with a slit. Behind his back, he wears an axe, “cut off more than one head”, with a blade in a leather case.

In the executioner's craft, Walter values "severe justice" the most. He is proud that he always carefully reads the materials of the court and “never in his life executed a criminal whose guilt he doubted.” He does not like the interrogation part of the craft, because the execution is “full of nobility and severe greatness”, and torture “exposes all the lowest”. According to him, he became the executioner “for the sake of achieving fair punishments, according to the precepts of Pramatery Yumin.” He believes that the convict should be acquainted in advance and look into his eyes: “The offender is a man, not a pig at the slaughter.”

At a terrible look, Walter is soft-hearted. He regrets the condemned dog, "poor Kitty", and generally "forever regrets someone". He is fond of babies and mothers; his own children have already become young, and he misses “babies in diapers.” The girls in the brothels love him for the "aura of harsh masculinity", and he knows how to talk to them without any tools.

## Meeting with Mac

Walter first appears in the last chapter of The Great Tree Shadow. ([[ten-velikogo-dreva|The Shadow of the Great Tree]]) On the eve of the Day of Descent, December 1, 1775, in the inn "one of the few remaining quiet counties in the world," he found himself at the same table with Mac. He settled in the city in early September, buying a house with a balcony on Maple Street. According to the master movement, which Walter broke chicken wings in the joints, Mac suspected an interrogator of him. Walter "not without pride" called himself an executioner. Mack said that he “served in the same department”, and now retired and begins his business. Walter admitted that he was invited to another city to complete the process, but the sentence is “slippery”: it is unclear whether it will be execution or murder. Mac called him in. On the morning of December 2, Walter became the first client of the McFried Crowe office. Investigations and Legal Services.”

His new friend is by all appearances [[mark-voron|Mark]], the former Crow of the Crown, who resigned in August 1775. Mac "lived in Faunterra for many years", hiding a scar on his hand under his cuff, was a defendant and underwent torture interrogations, hates the sea. He recalls Northwood's "Bear Lady" and the "terribly intelligent but fantasizing" girl he "crossed over the gallows" with and whom he initially suspected.

## In "Fantasies"

### The Routine Affair (1776)

In Fantasies, which the author calls possible futures, Walter and Mac work together. In the summer of 1776, the mayor of the free city of [[finboro|Finborough]], Alois Brickman, invited Walter to cut off the prisoner's head, but attached to the letter only a deposit, without a copy of the sentence. Walter suspected that they wanted to use him for reprisals, and persuaded Mack to go together, dividing the fee in half: “Money is good, but conscience is more expensive.”

A red dog named Kusaka was convicted of biting Baron Reginald Deveaux. ([[barony-devo|The Barons Devo]]) Mack checked the records and admitted that the sentence was legal, but Walter could not accept it. The owner of the dog, a fisherman Finch, refused: “I am a man of the law!” Together with Finch, he asked the Baron to withdraw the charge, but was refused. In the brothel, Walter himself conducted a survey of girls and mistresses and was the first to guess who of the girls served the baron. Mac's investigation revealed that Reginald killed Bernard's older brother, hid the body in the waste pipe, and the dog misled him because he found the corpse. Kusaka was acquitted. Walter ended up pitying Kitty, the victim's mistress, and Mac asked him, "How did you even become a executioner?"

### "Deadly Evidence" (1777)

In June 1777, Walter was summoned to Winslow, thirty miles from Grace: the coachman Pelmon, sentenced to be burned for the murder of Farnsworth's father, stabbed in a closed carriage, pleaded not guilty. Baron Winslow demanded a confession to replace the bonfire with a gallows and not disturb the nursing mother, Lady Valerie Gracend, "my seigneur's daughter". ([[valeri-grejsend|Valerie Graysend]]) This time Mack was the executioner's assistant. Walter slightly "blurred" the coachman with a club and received a confession, but Mac, doubting, refused to sign the protocol and persuaded the baron to give them two more days.

Walter visited Lady Valerie. She received him cordially, talked for half a day about motherhood and showed him doubles; Walter blessed the baby: “May you never again be touched by the hand of a man of my craft.” Her version that everything is the fault of the northerners, who took the family of the coachman hostage, he enthusiastically told Mak and was offended when he called Valerie “Smooth Bulka”. From Mac, he first heard about the secret order and the Great Tree. Walter was afraid to execute an innocent for the first time in his life, and the honor of the executioner did not allow him to take the convict's things from the dungeon. Mac eventually found out that Farnsworth, having decided that he was poisoned, stabbed himself and hid the sheath, and the coachman really replaced his flask with a poisoned one. Pelmon's confession had already been recorded, and a gallows was waiting for him instead of a fire.
